Will this water point still work?

We rank about 157,000 rural water points in Uganda, Kenya, Tanzania and Ethiopia by their risk of having no water on the survey day, using only attributes that do not depend on the point's current status. Then we ask: how much of that skill is lost when the model has never seen the country it is used in?

ES 335 Machine Learning, IIT Gandhinagar · Beli Dedhia & Poojan Soni · November 2026 · Code on GitHub

With local data, the riskiest 10% of points flagged by the final model have 1.5–3.8 times the overall no-water rate. A model trained only on other countries does much worse; in Ethiopia it ranks points backwards. Local data is the single biggest lever.

The question

A district water office cannot visit every hand pump, borehole and tap each year. If it could rank points by risk, a crew with time for, say, 10% of points could go to the most likely failures first. The prediction target is WPdx's status_id = "No": no water at the point on the visit day. It is recorded the same way in all four countries, unlike the free-text status, which is not.

Prediction time. The model sees only static attributes known before a visit: age, water source, technology, management type, local population, distances to roads and towns, and an urban flag. The label is what the visit found. This is a cross-sectional ranking problem, not forecasting: each point appears once, at its latest survey.

Why four countries. Ministries often have survey data from elsewhere but not their own district. So the second question is about transfer: does a model trained on other countries' surveys rank points in a new country as well as one trained on local data?

Data

Water Point Data Exchange Plus (WPdx+), CC BY 4.0, downloaded 6 November 2026. One row per water point at its latest survey.

What a full-column audit found before any modelling

We profiled every one of the 74 columns before training a model. Script: src/audit_data.py; report: results/audit/data_audit.md.

  • Leakage. Several columns nearly give the answer away. water_point_history separates working from broken points with AUC 0.99, the raw status text 0.96, and status_clean is the label in other words. All are excluded. So are the dates and survey IDs: source alone scores 0.85 in Ethiopia because different surveys found very different failure rates.
  • Missing values are not random. In Kenya, half the rows come from one provider whose points are 95% missing an install year and only 1.6% broken. In Ethiopia, the Ministry's rows are 100% missing a year and 38% broken. "Year is missing" therefore predicts the label (AUC 0.67 in Kenya, 0.63 in Ethiopia), but it is predicting who did the survey, not how the pump is doing. We keep the missing-value flags in the main models and remove them in an ablation.
  • The country is recoverable from the features. A boosted-tree classifier names the country from our input features alone with 97.5% accuracy (majority baseline 60%). So "transfer to an unseen country" is really transfer to an unseen survey, and we word every conclusion that way.
  • Spatial clustering. District identity alone predicts the label (AUC 0.65–0.76), so neighbouring points are not independent. This decides the split below.

Preprocessing

Every transform lives inside a scikit-learn Pipeline and is fitted on the training folds only, so no statistic from validation or test points leaks into training (src/features.py).

  • Distances and population are heavy-tailed (a few points are 100+ km from a road), so they get log1p. This matters for linear models and SVMs, whose loss is dominated by extreme values; trees split on order and do not care.
  • Scaling (z-scores) only for logistic regression and SVMs: the regularisation penalty and the RBF kernel distance both assume features on comparable scales.
  • Categoricals are one-hot encoded. Levels with fewer than 30 training rows are merged into "infrequent", and unseen levels at test time are handled, not crashed on.
  • Missing values: median imputation for numbers plus a 0/1 "was missing" column; a "missing" level for categories. The no-missing-signal ablation imputes without any flag, so no model, not even XGBoost (which would otherwise learn from NaN), can see that a value was absent.
  • Install year is set to missing when before 1950 or after the survey year (368 values). WPdx's own duplicate flag removes 1.1% of rows.

Evaluation design

Test set: whole districts, frozen before any model was trained

Random row splits put a pump's neighbours in training and the pump in test, so the model is graded on places it has effectively seen. Our concept experiment below measures the effect: on Uganda, random 3-fold CV says PR-AUC 0.43, but holding out whole districts says 0.28. So, per country, we hold out whole districts (district = region + district name, because the same district name occurs in two regions) covering about 20% of rows. Among 5,000 seeded random draws, we pick the one whose no-water rate is closest to the country's, which is a stratified group split. Districts with more than 15% of a country's rows are never test districts; Ethiopia's largest has 29.6%. The list was committed to the repository (splits/test_districts.json) before any model was fitted.

Three training regimes, one test set

  • A · pooled: the other three countries plus the target's training districts.
  • B · foreign only: the other three countries. The model has never seen the target country.
  • C · local only: the target's training districts.

All three are scored on the same test districts, so differences between them are paired. Regime C was not in the original plan. We added it after the first run (see the amendment), when pooled logistic regression ranked Ethiopia backwards.

Metrics

  • Primary: PR-AUC (average precision) of the no-water class. A crew acts on the top of the ranking, and PR-AUC measures exactly how pure the top is. A random ranking scores the no-water rate, which runs from 9% (Kenya) to 43% (Tanzania), so raw PR-AUC cannot be compared across countries.
  • Comparing countries: skill, not lift. Lift = PR-AUC ÷ no-water rate is intuitive ("2× better than random"), but its ceiling is 1 ÷ no-water rate: 11× in Kenya, only 2.3× in Tanzania. A perfect Tanzanian model would look worse than a mediocre Kenyan one. So across countries we use skill = (PR-AUC − rate) ÷ (1 − rate): 0 = random, 1 = perfect, whatever the base rate. It is the same normalisation our tuning score uses.
  • Why not accuracy? In Kenya, "every point works" is 91% accurate and finds zero broken points. Accuracy rewards the majority class, so it is useless for ranking.
  • Also: ROC-AUC; precision and recall when a crew inspects the top 5/10/20% (tie-aware: the one-rule stump gives only two scores, so ties are broken at random in expectation); Brier score and a reliability curve for calibration.

Tuning and uncertainty

Hyperparameters are chosen by grouped 3-fold cross-validation over training districts only. The test districts are scored once, at the end. The tuning score is computed within each country and averaged. A pooled score over a fold that mixes Tanzania (43%) with Kenya (9%) mostly rewards telling the countries apart, which is not the task.

For uncertainty we resample test districts (not rows) 2,000 times. Because two regimes are scored on the same districts, we bootstrap the difference directly (a paired bootstrap). Checking whether two separate confidence intervals overlap is a much weaker test.

Amendment (6 Nov). The first run gave pooled logistic regression an Ethiopia ROC-AUC of 0.38, worse than random. Its coefficients flip sign between the pooled fit and an Ethiopia-only fit. For example, a missing install year means low risk in Kenya, where most such points come from one well-performing provider, but high risk in Ethiopia, where they come from the Ministry. A single set of coefficients cannot fit both, so the pooled model buries the local signal (a Simpson's-paradox pattern). We added regime C. This diagnostic scored the test districts once, but the decision can be made on training districts alone: in-sample ROC-AUC is 0.84 for an Ethiopia-only fit against 0.67 pooled. Test districts, features, metrics and tuning did not change.

Results

Headline. In every country, the final model ranks water points better than the one-rule baseline and better than the best model that never saw the country, with 95% district-bootstrap intervals that exclude zero for both comparisons. The size of the gain varies a lot:

  • Ethiopia: test PR-AUC 0.41 vs a 10.6% no-water rate (3.8× random). The best foreign-only model scores 0.08, worse than random.
  • Tanzania: 0.58 vs 43.4% (skill 0.26). Of the top 10% flagged, 67% had no water.
  • Uganda: 0.34 vs 17.3% (1.9×).
  • Kenya: 0.19 vs 8.7% (2.2×), the weakest skill (0.11). Kenya's failures are rare and spread thin.

These are honest, moderate numbers. With only static attributes and one survey day per point, much of what decides whether a pump works on a given day (rainfall, groundwater, spare parts, a committee that collects fees) is simply not in the data.

A vs B vs C: what local data is worth

Paired bootstrap of the regime differences (table)

Local data beats foreign data in every country. Local-only training (C) beats foreign-only training (B) for every model in every country, with one exception (Kenya's RBF SVM). Adding foreign data to local data (A) does not help: pooled training is close to local-only in Uganda, which is 60% of the pooled rows anyway, far below it in Ethiopia (15% of rows), and in Tanzania it is even worse than foreign-only for 4 of the 7 models. A model fitted to everyone fits the largest surveys best, and when a feature means the opposite thing in a small country (missing install year: safe in Kenya, risky in Ethiopia), the small country loses. Trees can in principle learn country-specific branches, but country is not an input, so they have to discover it through location proxies.

How sure are we? Paired district bootstrap (2,000 resamples), over the four main tuned models (logistic regression, tree, random forest, XGBoost) × four countries = 16 comparisons each:

  • C − B (local only vs foreign only): positive in all 16, and the 95% interval excludes zero in 14. The two exceptions are logistic regression and the tree in Tanzania, which has only 16 test districts.
  • A − C (does adding foreign data help?): never significantly positive. Its estimate is negative in all 16 and significantly negative in 8 (Ethiopia's logistic regression, random forest and XGBoost; Kenya's logistic regression; all four in Tanzania).
  • Against the one-rule stump: every local-only model beats it significantly (16/16); pooled models 9/16; foreign-only 9/16.

Use the table under the chart for every interval.

Simpler models win on less data. With local data only, grouped CV chose depth-3 XGBoost in every country (depth 6 when pooled) and stronger regularisation for logistic regression in Ethiopia (C = 0.01). This is the bias–variance trade-off: fewer rows, so a simpler hypothesis class generalises better.

Calibration

When the model says 30%, are 30% of those points broken? The dashed diagonal is perfect calibration. Platt scaling is a one-feature logistic regression fitted on the model's out-of-fold predictions (training districts only). It is monotone, so the ranking and PR-AUC do not change; only the probabilities do.

Which inputs matter (permutation importance)

Shuffle one column across test points, re-run the full pipeline, measure the PR-AUC lost. A categorical counts once, not per dummy.

What CV chose. Local random forest in Ethiopia, local logistic regression in Tanzania, and the stacked ensemble in Kenya and Uganda. In Tanzania, CV preferred logistic regression (0.657) over local XGBoost (0.644). On the test districts XGBoost turned out better (skill 0.34 vs 0.26), but the CV gap was far inside its own noise (±0.09). We report the CV choice anyway: picking the test winner would turn the test set into a validation set and make every reported number optimistic.

The stack. Its weights lean on the local models (local random forest +0.78 in Ethiopia, +0.80 in Uganda). Some weights are negative (local random forest −0.41 in Tanzania). That does not mean "this model is harmful": the base models are highly correlated, so the stacker uses differences between them. Stack weights are not feature importances.

Calibration. Platt scaling fitted on out-of-fold predictions learned slopes of 0.84–1.08. A slope of 1 means "already calibrated", so it changed little. It slightly helped Tanzania (Brier 0.234 → 0.232) and slightly hurt Ethiopia (0.078 → 0.080). Calibration learned on training districts does not fully carry over to new districts; Tanzania's bins miss by about 8 points on average. We kept the rule we fixed beforehand rather than switching after seeing test results.

What the models use. Permutation importance differs by country, which is itself evidence against one-size-fits-all transfer. Technology, management, water source and age matter in Ethiopia. Age dominates in Tanzania, where the audit had already found it was the strongest single feature. Local population matters most in Uganda, partly through its 17.5% missingness (see ablations). Water source matters most in Kenya.

Where the model is wrong

Error analysis for the country and crew size chosen above. A group where mean predicted risk is far from the observed rate is a group the model systematically misreads.

Most remaining error is about place, not pump. Averaged per test district, predicted and observed no-water rates agree well in Ethiopia (correlation 0.89) and Kenya (0.84) but poorly in Tanzania (0.36) and Uganda (0.25). The biggest misses are whole districts:

  • Ethiopia, Somali region: in Siti, 49% of points had no water, but the model predicted 23% on average. In Nogob, 56% vs 34%. These are arid lowlands, where seasonal drying is one plausible reason; rainfall and groundwater are not in WPdx.
  • Kenya, Kajiado South: 2% observed vs 20% predicted: the model flags it as risky, but its points work.
  • Uganda, Kitgum: 37% observed vs 22% predicted. Tanzania, Maswa: 24% vs 43%.
  • Missing management in Ethiopia: these 1,050 points are 34% broken but are predicted 17%. Missingness tracks the data provider, and providers differ between training and test districts.

This is the same spatial correlation that makes random CV optimistic: districts differ in ways the features cannot see. A district-level effect (for example, a hierarchical model) would need labelled points from the same district, which is exactly what a new district does not have.

Logistic regression from scratch: first-order vs second-order optimisation

We minimise the L2-regularised cross-entropy J(w,b) = −(1/n) Σ [y log p + (1−y) log(1−p)] + (λ/2)‖w‖², with gradient (1/n) Xᵀ(p − y) + λw. Gradient descent uses only the slope. Newton's method (IRLS) also uses the curvature, through the Hessian (1/n) Xᵀ diag(p(1−p)) X + λI, and jumps straight to the bottom of the bowl. Because the loss is convex, both reach the same optimum as scikit-learn, which solves the same problem with C = 1/(nλ).

Ablations: is the signal real?

  • No missing signal: does the model rank points by survey provider, through which values are missing?
  • No remoteness / population: these features locate a point, and the country is 97.5% recoverable from them. Does the local advantage survive without them?
  • + fee payment: whether users pay for water matters in the literature, but it is recorded almost only in Tanzania.

Measured as test skill (0 = random) for XGBoost and logistic regression in all three regimes:

  • No missing signal. Ethiopia's local XGBoost falls from 0.30 to 0.19 and Uganda's local logistic regression from 0.17 to 0.09. Part of the signal in those countries is which provider surveyed the point. Kenya and Tanzania barely move. Local models still beat foreign-only ones everywhere, so the local advantage is not only fingerprinting.
  • No remoteness or population. Uganda and Tanzania lose the most (local XGBoost 0.19 → 0.13 and 0.34 → 0.28): location features carry real signal there. The local-beats-foreign gap survives in every country.
  • + fee payment. It helps where it is recorded (Tanzania local XGBoost 0.34 → 0.37) and helps more in Uganda (0.19 → 0.28) and pooled Ethiopia. Payment is missing for 64% of Ugandan and 91% of Ethiopian points, so those gains may again partly be missingness. It is a lead for future data collection, not a clean causal finding.

Limitations

  • The label is one survey day. "No water" mixes broken pumps, seasonally dry sources and differences in survey practice. We predict what a survey would find, not mechanical failure.
  • Country = survey. The country is 97.5% recoverable from the features, and each country's data comes from a few providers and years (73% of Uganda's rows were reported in a single year). "Transfer to a new country" here means transfer to a new survey and era; we cannot separate the two.
  • Missingness is informative and provider-driven (missing not at random). It helps the models in Ethiopia and Uganda, and it would not help in a place surveyed differently. The ablation measures how much it contributes.
  • Few test districts in some countries (Tanzania 16, Ethiopia 33), so intervals are wide and one district can move a result. Ethiopia's largest district (29.6% of rows) is never tested.
  • No environmental features. Rainfall, groundwater depth and conflict are absent; the error analysis shows this is where the biggest misses are.
  • Cross-sectional, not a forecast. Static attributes and the label come from the same visit, so nothing here says when a working pump will fail.
  • One added regime. Regime C was added after the first run. Its reason is visible in training data alone, and nothing about the test set, features or tuning changed, but it was not planned in advance.

Conclusion

Static attributes rank rural water points by no-water risk better than a one-rule baseline in all four countries, and the gain is statistically clear at the district level. But the skill depends on having labelled data from the same country. A model trained on the other three countries' surveys does much worse, and ranks Ethiopia backwards. Pooling foreign data with local data does not fix this for small countries, because a single model fits the largest surveys and some features mean opposite things in different places.

For a ministry, the practical message is: label a sample of your own water points before trusting a model. In Ethiopia, Kenya and Tanzania, 14,000–19,000 local labels beat a model trained on 134,000–140,000 points from elsewhere. For the method, the lessons are the ones the course stresses, measured here: validate on held-out groups when data is clustered (random CV overstated PR-AUC by 50%), compare against a baseline with a paired test, choose models without looking at the test set, and check which "signal" is really the data-collection process.
