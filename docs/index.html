Will this water point still work?

We rank about 157,000 rural water points in Uganda, Kenya, Tanzania and Ethiopia by their risk of having no water on the survey day, using only attributes that do not depend on the point's current status. Then we ask: how much of that skill is lost when the model has never seen the country it is used in?

ES 335 Machine Learning, IIT Gandhinagar · Beli Dedhia & Poojan Soni · November 2026 · Code on GitHub

Main findings
  • The model works, modestly. In all four countries it ranks water points better than random and better than a one-rule baseline. A crew that visits the riskiest 10% finds dry points 1.5–3.8 times as often as by visiting at random.
  • Local labels are what matter. Models trained only on other countries do much worse, and rank Ethiopia worse than random. Adding foreign data helps only if the model is told the country, and even then it only matches training on local data alone.
  • Read the numbers with care. Ethiopia's strong result mostly comes from telling survey programs apart. Accuracy beats "every point works" clearly only in Tanzania (elsewhere by under 1 point), so use these models to order visits, not to label points yes or no.

With local data, the riskiest 10% of points flagged by the final model have 1.5–3.8 times the overall no-water rate. A model trained only on other countries does much worse; in Ethiopia it ranks points backwards.

Terms used on this page
No-water rate
Share of points with no water on the survey day (9% in Kenya to 43% in Tanzania). A random ranking finds dry points at exactly this rate.
PR-AUC
Average precision of the ranking: how pure the top of the list is, averaged over all list lengths. Random = the no-water rate; perfect = 1.
Lift
No-water rate among the points a crew visits ÷ the overall rate. 2× means a visit is twice as likely to find a dry point as a random visit.
Skill
(PR-AUC − no-water rate) ÷ (1 − no-water rate). 0 = random, 1 = perfect, whatever the base rate, so countries can be compared.
ROC-AUC
Chance that a random dry point is ranked above a random working one. 0.5 = random.
Regimes A, B, C
What the model was trained on: A = other countries plus the target's own training districts; B = other countries only; C = the target's own training districts only.
Test districts
Whole districts, about 20% of each country, held out before any model was trained and scored once at the end.
95% interval
From resampling whole test districts 2,000 times; the range a result moves over if we had drawn different districts.
Calibration
Whether a predicted 30% risk really means 30% of such points are dry.

The question

A district water office cannot visit every hand pump, borehole and tap each year. If it could rank points by risk, a crew with time for, say, 10% of points could go to the most likely failures first. The prediction target is WPdx's status_id = "No": no water at the point on the visit day. All four countries record it the same way, which is not true of the free-text status.

At prediction time the model sees only attributes that describe how a point was built and where it is: age, water source, technology, management type, local population, distances to roads and towns, and an urban flag. Age and remoteness are among the predictors earlier work linked to failed pumps [Foster 2013]. None of them measures whether water is flowing. In WPdx these attributes are recorded on the same survey visit as the label. Our use case therefore assumes that a ministry already holds them from an earlier inventory or construction record, and wants to decide where to send the next visit. The label is what the visit found. This is a cross-sectional ranking problem, not forecasting: each point appears once, at its latest survey.

That assumption has a risk. If survey teams record attributes differently when a point is dry (for example, leaving management blank), part of the signal would come from recording practice. Missing values are the most likely route for this, so the no-missing-signal ablation removes them, and the provider check measures how much of the ranking follows the survey.

We use four countries because ministries often have survey data from elsewhere but not from their own district. So the second question is about transfer: does a model trained on other countries' surveys rank points in a new country as well as one trained on local data?

Data

Water Point Data Exchange Plus (WPdx+), CC BY 4.0, downloaded 6 November 2026 [WPdx]. One row per water point at its latest survey.

What a full-column audit found before any modelling

We profiled every one of the 74 columns before training a model. Script: src/audit_data.py; report: results/audit/data_audit.md.

  • Several columns leak the label. water_point_history separates working from broken points with AUC 0.99, the raw status text 0.96, and status_clean is the label in other words. All are excluded. So are the dates and survey IDs: source alone scores 0.85 in Ethiopia because different surveys found very different failure rates.
  • Missing values are not random. In Kenya, half the rows come from one provider whose points are 95% missing an install year and only 1.6% broken. In Ethiopia, the Ministry's rows are 100% missing a year and 38% broken. "Year is missing" therefore predicts the label (AUC 0.67 in Kenya, 0.63 in Ethiopia), but what it predicts is who did the survey. We keep the missing-value flags in the main models and remove them in an ablation.
  • The country is recoverable from the features. A boosted-tree classifier names the country from our input features alone with 97.5% accuracy (majority baseline 60%). So "transfer to an unseen country" is really transfer to an unseen survey, and we word every conclusion that way.
  • 3,397 rows (2.1%) have status "Unknown" and are dropped, because they have no label. They are not spread evenly: 63% come from one provider, Evidence Action, whose labelled points are almost all working (1.6% no water in Kenya, 0% in Uganda), and Tanzania has none. Even in the extreme case where every Unknown point was working, the no-water rate would fall by at most 0.4 percentage points (Uganda; 0.1–0.3 in Ethiopia and Kenya), so dropping them cannot change the conclusions.
  • Points cluster in space. District identity alone predicts the label (AUC 0.65–0.76), so neighbouring points are not independent. This decides the split below.

Preprocessing

Every transform lives inside a scikit-learn Pipeline and is fitted on the training folds only, so no statistic from validation or test points leaks into training (src/features.py).

  • Distances and population are heavy-tailed (a few points are 100+ km from a road), so they get log1p. This matters for linear models and SVMs, whose loss is dominated by extreme values; trees split on order and do not care.
  • Only logistic regression and the SVMs get scaled inputs (z-scores): the regularisation penalty and the RBF kernel distance both assume features on comparable scales.
  • Categoricals are one-hot encoded. Levels with fewer than 30 training rows are merged into "infrequent". A level first seen at test time goes into that group when it exists, and otherwise gets all-zero columns. Why 30: at Kenya's 9% no-water rate, a level with 30 points has about 3 dry ones, so its coefficient or split would be fitted to two or three examples, which is pure variance.
  • Class imbalance (9–43% no water) is not reweighted. Our metrics judge the order of points, and class weights mainly shift predicted probabilities. We checked this with 5-fold grouped CV on each country's training districts: balanced weights for logistic regression and scale_pos_weight for XGBoost changed CV PR-AUC by at most 0.014, well inside the fold-to-fold spread of 0.03–0.11 (src/extra_checks.py).
  • Missing numbers get the median plus a 0/1 "was missing" column, and missing categories get a "missing" level. The no-missing-signal ablation imputes without any flag, so no model can see that a value was absent. That includes XGBoost, which would otherwise learn from NaN.
  • Install year is set to missing when before 1950 or after the survey year (365 values). WPdx's own duplicate flag removes 1.1% of rows.

Evaluation design

Test set: whole districts, frozen before any model was trained

Random row splits put a pump's neighbours in training and the pump in test, so the model is graded on places it has effectively seen [Roberts et al. 2017]. Our concept experiment below measures the effect: on Uganda, random 3-fold CV says PR-AUC 0.43, but holding out whole districts says 0.28. So, per country, we hold out whole districts (district = region + district name, because the same district name occurs in two regions) covering about 20% of rows. Among 5,000 seeded random draws, we pick the one whose no-water rate is closest to the country's, which is a stratified group split. Districts with more than 15% of a country's rows are never test districts; Ethiopia's largest has 29.3%. The list was committed to the repository (splits/test_districts.json) before any model was fitted.

Three training regimes, one test set

  • A · pooled: the other three countries plus the target's training districts.
  • B · foreign only: the other three countries. The model has never seen the target country.
  • C · local only: the target's training districts.

All three are scored on the same test districts, so differences between them are paired. Regime C was not in the original plan. We added it after the first run (see the amendment), when pooled logistic regression ranked Ethiopia backwards.

Metrics

  • The primary metric is PR-AUC (average precision) of the no-water class. A crew acts on the top of the ranking, and PR-AUC measures how pure the top is [Saito & Rehmsmeier 2015]. A random ranking scores the no-water rate, which runs from 9% (Kenya) to 43% (Tanzania), so raw PR-AUC cannot be compared across countries.
  • To compare countries we use skill. Lift = PR-AUC ÷ no-water rate is intuitive ("2× better than random"), but its ceiling is 1 ÷ no-water rate: 11× in Kenya, only 2.3× in Tanzania. A perfect Tanzanian model would look worse than a mediocre Kenyan one. Skill = (PR-AUC − rate) ÷ (1 − rate) is 0 for random and 1 for perfect, whatever the base rate. It is the same normalisation our tuning score uses.
  • We do not use accuracy. In Kenya, "every point works" is 91% accurate and finds zero broken points. Accuracy rewards the majority class, so it is useless for ranking.
  • We also report ROC-AUC; precision and recall when a crew inspects the top 5/10/20% (tie-aware: the one-rule stump gives only two scores, so ties are broken at random in expectation); and the Brier score and a reliability curve for calibration.

Tuning and uncertainty

Hyperparameters are chosen by grouped 3-fold cross-validation over training districts only. The test districts are scored once, at the end. The tuning score is computed within each country and averaged. A pooled score over a fold that mixes Tanzania (43%) with Kenya (9%) mostly rewards telling the countries apart, which is not the task.

For uncertainty we resample test districts (not rows) 2,000 times [Efron & Tibshirani 1993]. Because two regimes are scored on the same districts, we bootstrap the difference directly (a paired bootstrap). Checking whether two separate confidence intervals overlap is a much weaker test.

On 6 Nov we amended the plan and added regime C. The first run gave pooled logistic regression an Ethiopia ROC-AUC of 0.38, worse than random. Its coefficients flip sign between the pooled fit and an Ethiopia-only fit. For example, a missing install year means low risk in Kenya, where most such points come from one well-performing provider, but high risk in Ethiopia, where they come from the Ministry. A single set of coefficients cannot fit both, so the pooled model buries the local signal (a Simpson's-paradox pattern; in dataset-shift terms, the relationship between inputs and label changes between countries [Moreno-Torres et al. 2012]). This diagnostic scored the test districts once, but the decision can be made on training districts alone: in-sample ROC-AUC on Ethiopia's training points is 0.83 for an Ethiopia-only fit against 0.67 pooled. Test districts, features, metrics and tuning did not change.

On 15 Nov we amended the plan again. A review of our own design pointed out that regime A cannot see the country, so it is a weak test of pooling. We added pooled models that know the country (results): country as an input for trees and boosting, and country × every feature for logistic regression. We also ran the planned "Uganda subsampled" ablation, which had been dropped for time. The reason for both is visible without any test result. Test districts, features, metrics, tuning and the final-model choice did not change.

Choosing the final model without the test set

For each country, every candidate (5 models × regimes A and C, plus the stack) is scored by 5-fold grouped CV over that country's training districts. In each fold, the held-out districts are target-country districts, which is exactly what the test measures. The best CV score becomes the final model. All candidates are then scored on the test districts, and the table shows them all, but the test scores did not choose anything (src/final.py).

Results

In every country, the final model ranks water points better than the one-rule baseline and better than the best model that never saw the country, with 95% district-bootstrap intervals that exclude zero for both comparisons. The size of the gain varies a lot:

  • Ethiopia: test PR-AUC 0.41 vs a 10.6% no-water rate (3.8× random). The best foreign-only model scores 0.08, worse than random. Caveat: most of this skill comes from telling survey programs apart (see the provider check).
  • Tanzania: 0.58 vs 43.4% (skill 0.26). Of the top 10% flagged, 67% had no water.
  • Uganda: 0.34 vs 17.3% (1.9×).
  • Kenya: 0.19 vs 8.7% (2.2×), the weakest skill (0.11). Kenya's failures are rare and spread thin.

The gains are moderate. With only static attributes and one survey day per point, much of what decides whether a pump works on a given day (rainfall, groundwater, spare parts, a committee that collects fees) is not in the data.

How much better than random?

Lift = no-water rate among the riskiest 10% ÷ the overall rate (1× = random). Its ceiling is 1 ÷ the rate, so compare countries by skill. Intervals: 2,000 resamples of test districts. Last column: we shuffled each test set 10,000 times; 999 in 1,000 random orderings stay below this lift.

Every interval stays above random, and every model is far beyond what chance produces: the best of 10,000 random orderings reached a top-10% lift of only 1.17–1.63×. Tanzania's lift looks smallest only because 43% of its points are dry, so even a perfect model could reach just 2.3×; on skill, Tanzania is second. But see the survey-provider check before reading Ethiopia's 3.8× as knowledge about pumps.

What a crew would get

The crew inspects the riskiest share of this country's test points. "Found" counts dry points found; random inspection and the best foreign-only model are shown for comparison.

For example, in Uganda a crew visiting the riskiest 10% (1,943 points) finds 733 dry points, against 336 for random visits and 456 with the foreign-only model. It still misses 78% of the dry points, because most of them do not look risky from static attributes. In Kenya, only 1 in 5 of those trips finds a dry point: better than the 1 in 11 of random visits, but most trips still find water.

Why we do not report accuracy

Accuracy of a yes/no "no water" call. "Best possible" picks the threshold with hindsight on the test set, so it is an upper bound: a deployed model would have to fix its threshold in advance.

Apart from Tanzania, no threshold beats the trivial rule "every point works" by more than 1 point. In Ethiopia and Kenya the calibrated model gives almost no point a risk above 50% (Ethiopia's highest is 48%), so the 50% rule flags almost nothing and its accuracy equals the majority rate. In Uganda the 50% rule gains 0.8 points (83.6% against 82.7%), and even the best threshold, chosen with hindsight, adds at most 0.8 points in any of these three countries. Only in Tanzania, where 43% of points are dry, does the model clearly beat it (63.5% at 50%, 64.9% at best, against 56.6%).

This is expected. When dry points are rare and even the riskiest points are below 50% likely to be dry, the accuracy-maximising answer is "working" for almost everyone. A crew needs an order of visits, and the ranking metrics above measure that.

A vs B vs C: what local data is worth

Paired bootstrap of the regime differences (table)

Local data beats foreign data in every country. Local-only training (C) beats foreign-only training (B) for every model in every country, with one exception (Kenya's RBF SVM). Adding foreign data to local data (A) does not help when the model is not told the country: pooled training is close to local-only in Uganda, which is 55% of its pooled training rows anyway, far below it in Ethiopia (12% of its pooled rows), and in Tanzania it is even worse than foreign-only for 4 of the 7 models. A model fitted to everyone fits the largest surveys best, and when a feature means the opposite thing in a small country (missing install year: safe in Kenya, risky in Ethiopia), the small country loses. Trees can in principle learn country-specific branches, but country is not an input in regimes A–C, so they have to discover it through location proxies. The next section gives the pooled model the country.

To see how sure we are, we ran a paired district bootstrap (2,000 resamples) over the four main tuned models (logistic regression, tree, random forest, XGBoost) × four countries = 16 comparisons each:

  • C − B (local only vs foreign only) is positive in all 16, and the 95% interval excludes zero in 14. The two exceptions are logistic regression and the tree in Tanzania, which has only 16 test districts.
  • A − C asks whether adding foreign data helps a model that does not know the country. It is never significantly positive. Its estimate is negative in all 16 and significantly negative in 8 (Ethiopia's logistic regression, random forest and XGBoost; Kenya's logistic regression; all four in Tanzania).
  • Every local-only model beats the one-rule stump significantly (16/16); pooled models do in 9/16, and foreign-only models in 9/16.

These are 48 intervals, so a few could exclude zero by chance. We applied a Holm correction over all 48 [Holm 1979], using z-tests with each difference's bootstrap standard error. After the correction, C − B stays significant in 10 of 16 comparisons, including all four models in Ethiopia, and A − C is significantly negative in 4 of 16. No comparison is significant in the opposite direction, so every conclusion keeps its direction. The four C − B gaps that lose significance (Uganda's logistic regression, tree and random forest, and Kenya's logistic regression) are less certain than their raw intervals suggest (results/holm_main.csv).

Use the table under the chart for every interval.

Simpler models win on less data. With local data only, grouped CV chose depth-3 XGBoost in every country (depth 6 when pooled) and stronger regularisation for logistic regression in Ethiopia (C = 0.01). This is the bias–variance trade-off: fewer rows, so a simpler hypothesis class generalises better.

Does pooling work if the model knows the country?

Regime A gives the pooled model no way to tell countries apart, so "pooling does not help" could just mean "a model that cannot see the country does not help". We tested the direct fix (amendment, 15 Nov):

  • For trees, random forest and XGBoost, country becomes one more input, so a tree can branch on it and learn country-specific rules.
  • For logistic regression, a country indicator only shifts the intercept, and an intercept cannot flip a coefficient's sign. So we also fitted country × every feature: each country gets its own coefficients, and the L2 penalty shrinks them towards shared ones, so a small country borrows strength from the others (partial pooling).

Same frozen test districts as above. Whiskers and table: paired district bootstrap, 2,000 resamples; green or red = the 95% interval excludes zero.

  • Knowing the country helps the pooled model a lot. With country as an input, the pooled tree, forest and XGBoost beat plain pooled training in all 12 comparisons, significantly in 10 (Ethiopia's random forest: 0.19 → 0.39). Country × features does the same for logistic regression in Ethiopia, Kenya and Tanzania (Ethiopia: 0.10 → 0.30).
  • Then pooled matches local-only, but does not beat it. For the tree, forest and XGBoost, pooled-with-country minus local-only is between −0.025 and +0.016 PR-AUC in every country, and only one interval excludes zero (Uganda's forest, +0.007). The interaction logistic regression is within 0.014 of local-only in every country.
  • An intercept is not enough. Logistic regression with only a country indicator stays close to random in Ethiopia (0.12, against 0.31 local-only), because the coefficients themselves have to flip between countries.

The corrected conclusion: what matters is labelled local data, plus a model that can treat countries differently. With both, 134,000–140,000 extra foreign rows add nothing measurable for Ethiopia, Kenya or Tanzania, and they also do no harm. These models were added after the main run, so they answer the pooling question only and were not added to the final-model candidates below.

Calibration

When the model says 30%, are 30% of those points broken? The dashed diagonal is perfect calibration. Platt scaling is a one-feature logistic regression fitted on the model's out-of-fold predictions (training districts only). It is monotone, so the ranking and PR-AUC do not change; only the probabilities do.

Which inputs matter (permutation importance)

Shuffle one column across test points, re-run the full pipeline, measure the PR-AUC lost. A categorical counts once, however many dummy columns it has.

CV chose local random forest in Ethiopia, local logistic regression in Tanzania, and the stacked ensemble in Kenya and Uganda. In Tanzania, CV preferred logistic regression (0.657) over local XGBoost (0.644). On the test districts XGBoost turned out better (skill 0.34 vs 0.26), but the CV gap was far inside its own noise (±0.09). We report the CV choice anyway: picking the test winner would turn the test set into a validation set and make every reported number optimistic.

The stack's weights lean on the local models (local random forest +0.78 in Ethiopia, +0.80 in Uganda). Some weights are negative (local random forest −0.41 in Tanzania). That does not mean "this model is harmful": the base models are highly correlated, so the stacker uses differences between them. Stack weights are not feature importances.

Platt scaling fitted on out-of-fold predictions learned slopes of 0.84–1.08. A slope of 1 means "already calibrated", so it changed little. It slightly helped Tanzania (Brier 0.234 → 0.232) and slightly hurt Ethiopia (0.078 → 0.080). Calibration learned on training districts does not fully carry over to new districts; Tanzania's bins miss by about 8 points on average. We kept the rule we had fixed before seeing test results.

Permutation importance differs by country, which is more evidence against one model for every country. Technology, management, water source and age matter in Ethiopia. Age dominates in Tanzania, where the audit had already found it was the strongest single feature. Local population matters most in Uganda, partly through its 17.5% missingness (see ablations). Water source matters most in Kenya.

How much of the ranking is the survey provider?

WPdx records which organisation surveyed each point (source). It is never a model input, because it says more about survey practice than about pumps. We used it only afterwards, to check what the models learned.

Test points by survey provider (providers with at least 100 test points). "Ranking within provider" is the final model's ROC-AUC among that provider's points only (0.5 = random). "Provider only" ranks every point by its provider's test-set no-water rate: an optimistic upper bound for a model that knows only who did the survey. Red: within-provider ranking below 0.6.

In Ethiopia, most of the skill is telling survey programs apart. Three programs (the Ministry, USAID's Lowland WASH Activity and charity: water) hold 21% of Ethiopia's test points but 77% of its dry points, and 87% of the points the model flags in its top 20%. The largest program, COWASH, holds 76% of points, and only 3% of them are dry. Knowing only the provider would already give ROC-AUC 0.83, against the model's 0.87. Within each high-failure program, the model ranks only a little better than chance (ROC-AUC 0.51–0.62). The model has no source column, so it finds the program through the features that differ between programs: the Ministry's points are all missing an install year, and technology and management mixes differ.

So Ethiopia's 3.8× lift is real for a ministry holding the same mix of surveys, but it is mostly not knowledge about pumps, and it would not carry over to a district surveyed by a single program. The no-missing-signal ablation agrees: without missing-value flags, Ethiopia's local XGBoost loses about a third of its skill.

Elsewhere the provider explains much less. Knowing only the provider gives ROC-AUC 0.66 in Kenya, 0.54 in Tanzania and 0.59 in Uganda, well below the models' 0.72, 0.64 and 0.68. Within each of these countries' two largest providers, the model ranks at 0.63–0.84. In Uganda, among the Ministry's survey alone (73% of test points), it scores 0.66, almost its overall 0.68. Not every provider is ranked well: within some smaller Ugandan surveys (Water For People, C & D) the model is no better than chance.

The pattern is the same in every country. Confident false alarms are mostly hand pumps with no or missing management, or pumps decades old: the profile that is usually risky. Confident misses are mostly points with a missing technology record, or brand-new taps and rainwater systems: the profile that is usually safe. Static attributes cannot tell these cases apart. A new tap can still run dry, and an old hand pump can still work.

Most of the remaining error sits at the level of whole districts. Averaged per test district (districts with at least 20 test points, as in the table above), predicted and observed no-water rates agree well in Ethiopia (correlation 0.88) and Kenya (0.83), moderately in Tanzania (0.69, from only 11 districts, so it moves a lot with which districts are included) and poorly in Uganda (0.25). Logistic regression from scratch: first-order vs second-order optimisation

We minimise the L2-regularised cross-entropy J(w,b) = −(1/n) Σ [y log p + (1−y) log(1−p)] + (λ/2)‖w‖², with gradient (1/n) Xᵀ(p − y) + λw. Gradient descent uses only the slope. Newton's method (IRLS) also uses the curvature, through the Hessian (1/n) Xᵀ diag(p(1−p)) X + λI, and jumps straight to the bottom of the bowl. The loss is convex, so there is one optimum, the same one scikit-learn finds by solving the problem with C = 1/(nλ). Newton reaches it in 5 steps; gradient descent is still slightly short after 3,000 (largest coefficient gap 0.03), though its predictions are almost identical.

Training PR-AUC keeps climbing. For deep trees, held-out PR-AUC peaks early (round 32 at depth 6, round 69 at depth 12) and then falls, while depth-2 trees keep improving slowly for over 1,000 rounds; deeper trees widen the train–held-out gap. That is why depth, child weight and λ are tuned.

Ablations: is the signal real?

  • Removing the missing-value signal asks whether the model ranks points by survey provider, through which values are missing.
  • Removing remoteness and population asks whether the local advantage survives without them. These features locate a point, and the country is 97.5% recoverable from the full feature set.
  • Adding fee payment tests a predictor from earlier work: across 25,000 hand pumps in Liberia, Sierra Leone and Uganda, not collecting user fees was one of the factors linked to non-functioning pumps, alongside age and distance from the district capital [Foster 2013]. In WPdx, payment is recorded almost only in Tanzania.
  • Cutting Uganda to 21,000 training rows (about the size of each other country) asks whether Uganda, which is 60% of all rows, swamps the foreign data, and whether that is why foreign-only training fails.

Measured as test skill (0 = random) for XGBoost and logistic regression in all three regimes:

  • Without the missing-value signal, Ethiopia's local XGBoost falls from 0.30 to 0.19 and Uganda's local logistic regression from 0.17 to 0.09. Part of the signal in those countries is which provider surveyed the point. Kenya and Tanzania barely move. Local models still beat foreign-only ones everywhere, so the local advantage survives without fingerprinting.
  • Without remoteness and population, Uganda and Tanzania lose the most (local XGBoost 0.19 → 0.13 and 0.34 → 0.28): location features carry real signal there. The local-beats-foreign gap survives in every country.
  • Fee payment helps where it is recorded (Tanzania local XGBoost 0.34 → 0.37) and helps more in Uganda (0.19 → 0.28) and pooled Ethiopia. Payment is missing for 64% of Ugandan and 91% of Ethiopian points, so those gains may again partly be missingness. It is a lead for future data collection; it does not show that collecting fees keeps pumps working.
  • With Uganda cut to 21,000 rows, regimes A and B change by at most 0.03 PR-AUC for Ethiopia, Kenya and Tanzania, mostly within noise (results/uganda_sub_vs_main.csv), and foreign-only training still ranks Ethiopia worse than random (ROC-AUC 0.33–0.41 for the tuned models). So foreign-only training fails because the foreign surveys differ, not because one large country swamps them.

Limitations

  • The label is one survey day. "No water" mixes broken pumps, seasonally dry sources and differences in survey practice. We predict what a survey would find, not mechanical failure.
  • Country and survey are confounded. The country is 97.5% recoverable from the features, and each country's data comes from a few providers and years (73% of Uganda's rows were reported in a single year). "Transfer to a new country" here means transfer to a new survey and era; we cannot separate the two.
  • Missingness is informative and provider-driven (missing not at random). It helps the models in Ethiopia and Uganda, and it would not help in a place surveyed differently. The ablation measures how much it contributes.
  • Ethiopia's headline is mostly survey program. Within each high-failure program the model ranks at ROC-AUC 0.51–0.62, and knowing only the provider gives ROC-AUC 0.83, close to the model's 0.87 (provider check). Its 3.8× lift should not be read as pump-level knowledge.
  • Accuracy barely beats "every point works" except in Tanzania (by under 1 point elsewhere). The models can order visits, but their yes/no calls are no better than assuming every point works.
  • Some countries have few test districts (Tanzania 16, Ethiopia 33), so intervals are wide and one district can move a result. Ethiopia's largest district (29.3% of rows) is never tested.
  • There are no environmental features. Rainfall, groundwater depth and conflict are absent; the error analysis shows this is where the biggest misses are.
  • Static attributes and the label come from the same visit, so nothing here says when a working pump will fail. This is a cross-sectional ranking, not a forecast.
  • We made two amendments after the first run. Regime C (6 Nov) and the country-aware pooled models (15 Nov) were not planned in advance. Both are disclosed with reasons that do not depend on test results, and neither changed the test set, features, tuning or final-model choice.
  • We made many comparisons. After a Holm correction over 48 regime comparisons, 10 of 16 local-vs-foreign gaps stay significant; the direction of every conclusion holds, but single model-by-country gaps are less certain than their raw intervals suggest.

Conclusion

Static attributes rank rural water points by no-water risk better than a one-rule baseline in all four countries, and the gain is statistically clear at the district level. But the skill depends on having labelled data from the same country. A model trained on the other three countries' surveys does much worse, and ranks Ethiopia backwards. Pooling foreign data with local data only works if the model is told the country, because some features mean opposite things in different places; even then it only matches local-only training.

A ministry should label a sample of its own water points before trusting a model. In Ethiopia, Kenya and Tanzania, 14,000–19,000 local labels beat a model trained on 134,000–140,000 points from elsewhere. For the method, the lessons are the ones the course stresses, measured here: validate on held-out groups when data is clustered (random CV overstated PR-AUC by 50%), compare against a baseline with a paired test, choose models without looking at the test set, and check which "signal" is really the data-collection process. That last check matters here: Ethiopia's strongest result turned out to be mostly a survey-program effect.
